AI mathematics took up most of my HN reading today. I kept coming back to a question: what happens after a problem is solved, when the answer arrives faster than anyone can understand it?

## Proofs, errors, and what comes next

[OpenAI's mathematics update](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/) describes a batch of results and work on formalising proofs. Then there's [the revision history](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/history.md): a sign error led to three dependent manuscripts being withdrawn. Those two pages belong together. A checked proof is valuable, but checking part of a project doesn't establish the rest of it.

[Terence Tao's Math 2.0 thread](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/117395269325940185) gave me more to think about than the announcements. A first proof usually sets other work in motion: people explain it, simplify it, teach it, and find new questions. If solving the problem becomes cheap, those contributions still need time and recognition. [Scott Aaronson's post](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10169) and the [Association for Humanistic Mathematics statement](https://www.ahmath.org/statements) show how much disagreement there is about where this goes. I'd rather have a proof I can learn from than another entry on a leaderboard.

## The software I want to keep using

[Dan Cohen's essay on durable software](https://newsletter.dancohen.org/archive/the-slow-formation-of-durable-software/) was a welcome change of pace. His account of Zotero's development is about years of requirements and collaboration. Getting something to run is one thing; making it useful enough that people keep relying on it takes longer.

[Hundred Rabbits](https://100r.ca/site/home.html) is always a good rabbit hole: Uxn, small tools, preservation, and experiments with older languages. I also liked [this history of DVD menus](https://vale.rocks/posts/dvd-menus). There's a lot of imagination in those awkward little interfaces. Constraints aren't always something to escape.

## A few programming bookmarks

[Push Ifs Up and Fors Down](https://debasishg.github.io/blog/push-ifs-up-fors-down/) is one I'll return to. Move decisions toward the boundaries and think about collections as collections. Useful advice for organising code; I'd still want measurements before calling it an optimisation.

The [floating-point essay](https://blog.pkh.me/p/49-float-and-integer-arithmetic-follow-two-different-paradigms.html) makes a distinction that's easy to blur: detecting a non-finite result and knowing whether a finite result is accurate are different jobs.

[GitHub's pull-request controls](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-02-13-new-repository-settings-for-configuring-pull-request-access/) let maintainers restrict who opens PRs, or disable them altogether. I can see why some projects would want that. [Docker Agent](https://github.com/docker/docker-agent) also caught my eye—YAML configuration, multiple model providers, and tools through MCP. I'd want a close look at the permissions and telemetry before putting it to work.

## Away from the announcements

[MIT's obituary for Margaret Hamilton](https://news.mit.edu/2026/margaret-hamilton-computing-pioneer-dies-1007) was the day's most memorable read. Apollo software puts a lot of today's talk about reliability into perspective.

[The search for Salvage](https://shahidhussain.com/writing/search-for-salvage/) was another favourite: a music mystery, an unhelpful name, and eventually the right people to ask. I won't spoil the route to the answer.

For something lighter, there's [Bigwords](https://bigwords.page/) and a collection of [ASCII animations](https://ascii.rest/). A browser can still be a place for a clever little toy. After all the arguments about the future of research, that was a pleasant place to finish.
